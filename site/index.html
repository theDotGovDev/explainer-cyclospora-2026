Is it safe to eat?

Practical, source-cited risk estimates for the 2026 U.S. Cyclospora outbreak — food by food, with what actually reduces the risk and by how much.

The short version: one recalled product drove this outbreak — iceberg lettuce from Taylor Farms de Mexico, pulled from the market on 2026-07-17. If you are not eating recalled product, your risk from any single food below is well under 1 in 10,000. Washing does not work on this parasite. Cooking and peeling do.

What actually works

Cyclospora does not behave like the bacteria most food-safety advice is written for. Two things work well, one thing barely works at all.

Cooking Works

Cooking to an internal temperature of about 158 F (70 C) or higher inactivates Cyclospora. Thorough cooking is the single most reliable consumer-level control.

Peeling Works

Peeling physically removes the contaminated outer surface and is highly effective, provided the knife and hands do not transfer oocysts to the flesh.

Avoiding recalled product Works

Removing the implicated supply is the single largest effect available, because it removes exposure entirely rather than reducing the dose. Check products against current FDA recall listings.

Washing Barely helps

Cyclospora oocysts are highly resistant to chlorine and to routine chemical disinfection. Commercial produce washes, vinegar soaks and chlorine rinses are not reliably effective. The oocyst wall is tough and lodges in microscopic surface crevices, so it does not rinse off cleanly. CDC still recommends rinsing produce under running water, but describes disinfection as unlikely to fully eliminate Cyclospora.

Cyclospora also does not spread person-to-person in any meaningful way. Cyclospora is not transmitted directly person-to-person in a meaningful way. Freshly passed oocysts are not infectious and require days to weeks in the environment to sporulate, so household spread from an ill person is not a significant route.[10][12][30][24] Sourcing: agency + news

What the evidence does and does not show

Separating confirmed findings from open questions and from historical associations is the most decision-relevant part of this page. Expand any item to read it in full.

No product sample has tested positive for Cyclospora

The link between iceberg lettuce and this outbreak rests on epidemiologic and traceback evidence, not on a laboratory detection in food. A border sample initially flagged as positive was re-reviewed by FDA laboratory experts on July 19, 2026 and reclassified as a false positive - FDA concluded the finding did not represent true amplification. FDA stated this did not change the basis for the investigation or the recall, which rest on the epidemiologic data. Cyclospora is notoriously difficult to detect in food, so the absence of a positive sample is weak evidence of absence.[31] Sourcing: news

There is more than one outbreak happening at once

FDA has described four separate Cyclospora outbreak investigations in 2026. Some are tied to the large Midwest iceberg lettuce cluster, some involve single states, and many reported cases are not yet linked to any cluster. The iceberg lettuce recall therefore does not account for all illness this season.[22][25] Sourcing: news

Case counts in circulation are inconsistent

Published counts differ depending on whether they describe the whole 2026 domestically acquired season or only the iceberg lettuce cluster, and on the date of the snapshot. Outbreak-cluster counts reported in July and August 2026 ranged from roughly 1,947 illnesses in 9 states (with 98 hospitalizations) to roughly 6,358 illnesses in 15 states (with 278 hospitalizations). Treat any single number as a dated snapshot, not a current total.[28][32] Sourcing: news

Other commodities are under active traceback

As of late July 2026, traceback in some of the 2026 investigations was reported to be focused on cilantro, onions and cucumbers. Public health officials in North Carolina indicated fresh parsley and cilantro may be behind the rise in cases in that state. These are open lines of inquiry, not conclusions.[22][24] Sourcing: news

What the status labels mean

  • Confirmed - implicated 2026 Named by FDA/CDC as implicated in the 2026 outbreak on epidemiologic and traceback evidence.
  • Under traceback Reported to be under active traceback or flagged by state officials in a 2026 investigation. Not a conclusion.
  • In recall scope Not independently implicated, but within the scope of the recall because it may contain the implicated ingredient.
  • Historical only Associated with prior U.S. cyclosporiasis outbreaks, but not implicated in 2026.
  • Not implicated No association in the 2026 outbreak and no strong historical association.

When to seek care

Cyclosporiasis does not reliably resolve on its own and is treated with prescription antibiotics, so it is worth getting diagnosed rather than waiting it out. Standard stool tests do not always look for Cyclospora — it often needs a specific request or a multiplex GI panel, so mention recent produce exposure to your clinician.

Seek care promptly

  • Watery diarrhea lasting more than a few days
  • Diarrhea that improves, then relapses
  • Signs of dehydration — dizziness standing up, little or no urination
  • Fever alongside diarrhea
  • Any diarrheal illness if you are immunocompromised, pregnant, elderly, or caring for a young child

Call 911

  • Severe dehydration
  • Fainting or collapse
  • Bloody stool

Symptoms usually begin about one week after infection, with a range of roughly 2 days to 2 weeks or more. Untreated illness can last from a few days to a month or longer, and relapsing symptoms are common. This section describes when to consult a professional.
